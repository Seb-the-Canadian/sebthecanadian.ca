An honest re-examination of my 2023 MA thesis, built in the open, and the accessible story that will come out of it. This page changes as the work does. Every change is dated at the bottom.

## Why I'm looking again

In 2023 I completed my Masters degree in sociology. What I didn't know then and what I know now is that you can get things wrong and still get a degree. Statistics were never my strong suit, and while rereading my thesis reaffirms that for me, it also makes me wonder if I'd not muddled through the numbers in grad school, would I be where I am today? Who knows. However, I do know that questions about structural inequality, the role of institutions, the people who move through them, and the ways those things interact are still ones that I think about regularly (and in my opinion are still very worth asking).

As such, with the help of Claude, I hope to review my thesis, tell the story of the process, make the information more accessible, and figure out where I went wrong (and how I might change things from here).

## What I asked in 2023

My thesis, *Down and Out: Support Services, Satisfaction, and the Impacts of Marginality Among Canadian Youth Experiencing Homelessness*, asked a simple question: are young people experiencing homelessness all served equally well by the services meant to help them? And if not, do marginalized identities, such as being Indigenous or LGBTQ2S+, help explain the difference?

To answer it, I used Canada's 2015 national youth homelessness survey. It reached 1,103 young people through 57 agencies across the country. Youth who had used services such as shelters, food banks, health and mental health care, and employment programs were asked how helpful those services had been. I used regression models to look for patterns in their answers.

## What I can and can't claim for now

I'm starting from what was published: the thesis, its tables and appendices, and the survey's national report. I don't currently have the survey data itself. Working from the published material, I can:

- restate the published numbers accurately, recomputed from the thesis's own tables
- read the published results correctly, in plain language
- sort every claim the thesis makes: supported, unsupported, contradicted, or unknowable from what was published
- describe the study I'd design now
- explain the statistical ideas with invented, clearly labelled data

What the published material can't do is support corrected findings, or confirm anything that would need the raw data. Where something is my best explanation rather than something I can check, I'll say so.
